How much of India's economy do Adani and Ambani hold?

Part 1 measures concentration, meaning the share of each market the two groups hold, sector by sector, on official totals, and their weight in the economy as a whole. Parts 2 and 3 take up how the assets were obtained and how they are financed.

1. The question and the answer in brief

Public argument about the Adani and Reliance groups tends to run on two kinds of evidence, lists of what each group owns, and the wealth of the two men who control them. Neither says how much of any market the groups hold. A list of assets cannot tell a 3% share from a 30% one, and a fortune moves with share prices. This paper asks the narrower question that can be answered from public records. In each market where these groups are large, what share do they hold, measured against an official total, and how has it changed?

The answer differs by group and by market, which is the first thing worth knowing. Adani's position is a set of large but minority shares across infrastructure, with 27% of port cargo, 23% of airport passengers, 24% of private coal power capacity, 9% of renewable capacity, 4% of the high-voltage grid, about 16% of cement capacity and 6% of household gas connections. In ports, coal power and cement, most of the growth since 2014 came from buying assets other companies had built. Reliance's position is narrower and deeper, with 41% of mobile subscribers and 45% of mobile revenue in a market now held largely by two firms, and 26% of refining capacity, a share that has been falling.

No market in Part 1 is a monopoly, and the strongest form of the public claim, that two groups control the economy, is not supported by these figures. A weaker claim is supported. In ports, private coal power and mobile telecom, one of these groups now holds a quarter or more of the market, and in each the share has risen sharply within a decade.

2. What is measured, and how

Every share in this paper is a numerator from the group's own filings, or from a regulator's table that names the company, over a denominator from a government source, which is the Ministry of Ports for cargo, the Airports Authority for passengers, the Central Electricity Authority for power and transmission, TRAI for telecom, the Petroleum Planning and Analysis Cell for refining and city gas, and the Economic Survey for cement. Where a company publishes its own market share, it is compared with the calculation and the difference is reported.

Four choices run through the paper and each moves the numbers.

  • Capacity or output. Power, refining and cement are measured by installed capacity, because that is what the government publishes by company. Capacity is not production, and it is not sales, which is what a competition authority usually measures.
  • Operated or owned. Companies report subsidiaries and joint ventures in full whatever their stake. An Adani company that holds half a joint venture reports all of its capacity. This paper uses the reported figures and says "runs" or "operates"; an ownership-weighted share would be lower. The one exception is city gas, where the joint venture with Indian Oil is shown separately.
  • National or regional. Every share here is national. Cement, city gas and power are sold in regional markets, where a group's share can be far higher or lower than its national one.
  • Which Reliance. Reliance Industries is Mukesh Ambani's company. Reliance Power and Reliance Infrastructure belong to his brother Anil Ambani's separate group and are left out throughout.

Every number printed here is computed from the tables in data/paper, where each row names its source document, publisher, address and page or table. Rows marked as derived are calculations made for this paper.

3. Adani in infrastructure

3.1 Ports

The share rose in 10 of the 12 years. The two steepest years were 2020-21 and 2021-22, when it went from 16.9% to 23.6%, the years Krishnapatnam, Dighi and Gangavaram joined the group. Most of the rise came from buying ports other companies built and ran, starting with Dhamra in June 2014, Kattupalli in June 2018, Krishnapatnam in October 2020, Dighi in February 2021, Gangavaram in stages to April 2021, Karaikal in April 2023 and Gopalpur in October 2024. Mundra, the group's first port, remains its largest.

The company's own share claims match this calculation for the last three years. For 2020-21 the company says 25%; on the same official denominator its own cargo figure gives 19.8%.

3.2 Airports

Adani took over Ahmedabad, Lucknow and Mangaluru from October 2020, Mumbai on 13 July 2021, and Jaipur, Guwahati and Thiruvananthapuram later in 2021. Navi Mumbai opened in 2025-26. Since 2022-23, the first full year with all seven, the share has stayed between 22.7% and 23.5%.

The share fell from 28.1% to 23.2% before Adani ran any of them, because traffic grew faster at other airports. What changed after 2020 is who runs them. One company now operates airports handling close to a quarter of passenger movements, Mumbai's among them. Six of these airports came from one privatisation round in 2019. The Ministry of Civil Aviation records 32 bids from 10 bidders, with the per-passenger fee as the bid, and Adani the highest bidder at all six. A group of secretaries decided on 17 November 2018 to set no cap on the number of airports one bidder could win, citing the six airports' 9.5% share of traffic. How the round was decided is the subject of Part 2.

3.3 Coal power

Private coal and lignite capacity grew fast to 2017-18 and then stopped. It was 75,546 MW in 2017-18 and 74,892 MW in 2025-26. Over the same years Adani Power went from 10,440 MW to 18,110 MW. The share therefore traced a U. It was 18.9% in 2013-14, fell to 13.6% by 2018-19 while others built, and rose to 24.2% as Adani bought plants at Udupi in April 2015, Raipur and Raigarh in 2019-20, and Mahan in March 2022. Godda, 1,600 MW in Jharkhand, was built by the group and sells its power to Bangladesh.

Of all coal and lignite capacity, public and private, Adani Power's share is 7.9%. The group also mines coal. Its companies won 10 of the 86 blocks sold in the Ministry of Coal's commercial auctions to July 2023, and it runs further mines under contract for state and central utilities, which own them.

3.4 Renewable power

Adani Green ran 1,958 MW in 2017-18 and 19,294 MW in 2025-26, while renewable capacity excluding large hydro across India went from 69,022 MW to 223,273 MW.

The share is of a market growing very fast, so it is the clearest case in this paper of a group gaining share by building. Adani Green's larger figures, such as a 'locked-in portfolio', count capacity contracted but not running, and are not used.

3.5 Transmission

The Central Electricity Authority's list of inter-state transmission projects awarded through tariff-based competitive bidding names Adani as the executing company on 27 of 176 projects commissioned or under construction, 15.3% by count. Adani Energy Solutions' operational network was 1.5% of India's lines at 220 kV and above in 2015-16 and 3.8% in 2025-26. In the CEA's count, lines built by private companies and joint ventures of every owner were 9.0% of the network in 2025-26; the rest belongs to central and state government utilities.

3.6 Cement

The Adani family took control of Ambuja Cements and, through it, ACC from Holcim in September 2022. The group then bought Sanghi Industries (December 2023), Penna Cement (August 2024) and Orient Cement (April 2025). Consolidated capacity went from 67.5 million tonnes a year in March 2023 to 109 in March 2026.

UltraTech, the largest, had 196.8. Against the Economic Survey's figure of about 690 million tonnes for all of India, the two together hold roughly 44% of capacity and Adani about 16%. Those two shares are indicative only, because the national figure has no stated date and UltraTech's includes plants abroad. Cement is sold regionally, so national capacity shares say little about market power in any one region. The Competition Commission's orders on each acquisition would give regional shares; they could not be retrieved for this paper.

3.7 City gas

Adani Total Gas holds licences for 34 of the geographical areas the Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board has authorised, and its 50:50 joint venture with Indian Oil holds 19 more. The joint venture adds 1.5% of household connections and 4.7% of CNG stations; it is kept separate here because Indian Oil owns half of it. These shares have barely moved since 2020-21, with household connections between 6.2% and 6.3%, CNG stations between 6.6% and 7.9%. City gas is a sizeable business for the group without being a dominant one.

Across these seven markets the pattern is consistent. Adani holds between a twentieth and a quarter of each, and in ports, coal power and cement the share grew mainly through acquisition. Renewable power is the exception, built from scratch in a fast-growing market.

4. Reliance in telecom and fuel

4.1 Mobile telecom

In March 2016, before Jio's launch, TRAI listed 12 mobile licensees with subscribers; in March 2026 it listed 5, Jio among them, the others having merged or stopped reporting. The concentration index rose from 1,544 to 3,255 over the same years. The 2023 United States Merger Guidelines treat a market above 1,800 as highly concentrated; Indian competition law sets no such threshold.

Jio and Airtel between them now have 77% of subscribers. The rest is mostly Vodafone Idea, itself the 2018 merger of two operators, and the state-owned BSNL. Jio grew by building its own network. A complaint that its launch pricing was predatory went to the Competition Commission in 2017 (Case 03 of 2017); the order could not be retrieved for this paper.

4.2 Telecom revenue

Revenue tells the same story as subscribers with less noise, because an inactive SIM earns nothing. Jio's share went from 12.8% in 2017-18 to 33.5% a year later, and has stayed between 39.9% and 44.6% since. Airtel's has risen every year since 2018-19.

Jio paid for the spectrum it uses in the government's auctions, paying Rs 13,672 crore in the Oct-2016 auction; Rs 57,123 crore in the Mar-2021 auction; Rs 88,078 crore in the Jul-Aug-2022 (5G) auction; Rs 974 crore in the Jun-2024 auction.

4.3 Refining

Reliance's two Jamnagar refineries hold 25.5% of India's refining capacity on 1 April 2026. The share jumped in 2009 when the second refinery opened and has drifted down since as public sector refiners added capacity; Reliance's own capacity has not changed since 2018.

This is the one market in Part 1 where a Reliance share is falling. It also overstates Reliance's place in India's fuel market, because the second refinery sits in a special economic zone and mainly exports.

4.4 Retail and media

Reliance is also India's largest retailer by its own account and, since November 2024, the controlling owner of the media joint venture with Disney. Neither can be given a share here. No government body publishes the size of the organised retail market, and the retail segment's revenue in Reliance's accounts includes wholesale and device sales. The Competition Commission approved the Disney combination on 28 August 2024 subject to modifications the parties offered; its detailed order, which states market shares, could not be retrieved for this paper and will be added when it is.

5. Weight in the economy

In March 2020 the listed Adani companies were worth 1.2% of all companies on the NSE; in March 2022, 5.0%. After the Hindenburg Research report of 24 January 2023 the share fell to 3.6% by March 2023, and was 3.0% in March 2025.

Reliance Industries alone was worth between 2.7% and 6.8% of the market across these years. By the size of its balance sheet it is much the larger group. A 2025 working paper by Dasgupta and Jayadev, using CMIE Prowess, puts Reliance at 11.3% of the assets of India's non-financial companies in 2023-24 and Adani at 2.5%, fourth among the five largest groups behind Tata and Aditya Birla.

The two measures answer different questions. Market value is what investors expect the companies to earn; it rose fastest for Adani and fell fastest after January 2023. Assets measure what the companies hold now, and on that measure Reliance is several times the size of the Adani group.

6. What others have found

The best-known estimate is Acharya (2023). Using CMIE Prowess, he finds that the five largest groups (Reliance, Tata, Aditya Birla, Adani and Bharti) held about 10% of non-financial corporate assets in 1991 and nearly 18% in 2021, that they first spread across sectors and then, from 2015, deepened their shares within them, and that their markups rose relative to other firms. He describes the link to inflation as potentially causal and calls for further inquiry. His sector figures for telecom, retail and petroleum are shares of the top five firms in each sector, and are often quoted as if they were Reliance's alone; they are not.

Commander, Estrin, Thomas and Lingineni (2026), the main peer-reviewed study, find that concentration within narrowly defined industries fell between 2000 and 2020, while markups rose after 2013 and business groups' revenue grew relative to GDP. The two findings fit together. A group can hold modest shares in many markets and still weigh more in the economy as a whole. That is close to what Part 1 finds for Adani.

Dasgupta and Jayadev (2025) are the only source found that reports the groups separately, and on assets they put Reliance at 11.3% and Adani at 2.5%. Their paper is a working paper and has not been peer reviewed. An earlier IMF study, Walton, Nath and Mody (2011), found little sign before about 2010 that Indian firms profited from product-market power, which places the rise these studies describe in the last decade. Markup estimates of the kind Acharya uses come from De Loecker, Eeckhout and Unger (2020); De Loecker and co-authors (2016) show, with Indian data, that markups can rise when costs fall and prices do not follow, so a rise in markups is a reason to investigate and does not by itself show market power.

7. How to read this

The analytic position of this paper is that concentration should be measured market by market, on official totals, before it is argued about. On that measure, the picture is of two different kinds of business house. Reliance holds large shares in a few markets that households pay into every month, namely telephone service, fuel and, by its own account, retail. Adani holds large minority positions in physical infrastructure, in ports, airports, power plants, transmission lines, coal mines and cement, much of it infrastructure the state once built and ran, or now allocates through licences, concessions and auctions.

The second pattern is the reason Parts 2 and 3 exist. A share built by acquisition and by winning concessions raises different questions from one built by taking customers from rivals. They are questions about the terms on which public assets were transferred, the competitive bidding that allocated them, and the lending that paid for them. Part 1 establishes how large the positions are. It does not say how they were obtained, and nothing here should be read as a finding about that.

8. Limits and next steps

  • The Competition Commission's detailed orders on the Reliance-Disney combination, the Holcim, Sanghi, Penna and Orient cement acquisitions, and the 2017 complaint against Jio could not be retrieved from outside India. They hold the Commission's own market definitions and shares and will be added.
  • The gas regulator's monthly reports and the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha answers on airport leases could not be retrieved for the same reason.
  • Capacity shares in power, refining and cement are not output or sales shares, and national shares are not regional ones.
  • Company figures count subsidiaries and joint ventures in full.
  • Most years for 2024-25 and 2025-26 are provisional and may be revised.
