How the Adani and Reliance assets were obtained

1. What a document can show

A government record can show who decided, when, by what process, what was bid or paid, what an official body objected to, and what a court held. It cannot show why a decision-maker chose as they did. Part 2 therefore reports processes and recorded findings, and makes no claim about motive. Three kinds of statement are kept apart throughout. They are decisions and holdings, which are facts about the record; findings by an auditor or regulator, which are that body's conclusions; and allegations, which are reported as allegations and never as findings.

Every event below is in the data tables with its document and page. Where a fact rests on a press report alone, the table says so and the text does not rely on it.

2. Airports and ports

2.1 Six airports in one round

In November 2018 the Union Cabinet approved leasing six Airports Authority airports (Ahmedabad, Jaipur, Lucknow, Guwahati, Thiruvananthapuram and Mangaluru) and set up an Empowered Group of Secretaries, chaired by the chief executive of NITI Aayog, to decide matters beyond the appraisal committee's remit. The Ministry of Civil Aviation records that this group decided on 17 November 2018 to put no limit on how many airports one bidder could win and to require no prior airport experience. Bidders needed Rs 3,500 crore of financial capacity and experience in any kind of infrastructure.

The Department of Economic Affairs and NITI Aayog both sent notes on the bid documents on 10 December 2018. The Kerala High Court's judgment summarises them. One asked for the bid parameter and the thresholds to be reviewed, the other said the two-stage procedure in the PPP guidelines had not been followed, that there was no approved model concession and no project cost estimate, and called the request for proposals "poorly prepared". The appraisal committee met the next day and the request for proposals went out on 14 December. The court held the notes to be recommendations that the committees could overrule and had considered. The Ministry's 2021 press note does not deny their content; it says they were not overruled because both departments sat on both committees.

Adani Enterprises quoted the highest fee per passenger at all six airports, by the Ministry's account. The only rival bid in a public record is at Thiruvananthapuram, where Adani bid Rs 168 per domestic passenger against Rs 135 from Kerala's state company, 20% lower and so outside the 10% band in which Kerala could have matched. The Cabinet approved three leases in July 2019 and three in August 2020, each for 50 years. By June 2024 the six concessionaires had paid the Airports Authority about Rs 2,032 crore in per-passenger fees and Rs 2,758 crore in upfront fees. The Kerala High Court dismissed the state's challenge in October 2020.

2.2 Mumbai and Navi Mumbai

Mumbai's airport came by acquisition. In August 2020 Adani Airport Holdings agreed to buy the debt of GVK's holding company, the route to GVK's 50.5% of Mumbai International Airport, and to complete the purchase of the 23.5% held by Airports Company South Africa and Bidvest. It took management control in July 2021 after approvals from the Union government, CIDCO and Maharashtra. Navi Mumbai's concession had been awarded in 2017, before Adani, to the GVK-led Mumbai company, which bid a 12.60% revenue share; the state auditor faulted signing it before the land was acquired. The CBI case against GVK and the Mumbai company, registered in June 2020, is reported in the press; no document in this record connects it to the sale.

2.3 Ports

The port record shows three routes. Some were negotiated purchases, such as 75% of Krishnapatnam at an enterprise value of Rs 13,572 crore in 2020, and Gopalpur in 2024. One was a sale by a state government, when Andhra Pradesh sold its 10.4% of Gangavaram to Adani Ports for Rs 644.78 crore in 2021, and no Government Order or valuation for that sale was found. Two came through insolvency, Dighi in 2020 and Karaikal in 2023, each on a resolution plan approved by the creditors and the tribunal.

Vizhinjam in Kerala was a concession, and the Comptroller and Auditor General audited it. Adani Ports was the lone bidder and asked for the full viability gap grant of Rs 1,635 crore; the state bears 67% of the cost. The auditor found that the 40-year term, ten years over the standard, gives the concessionaire Rs 29,217 crore of extra revenue, and that with the termination payment the state's net receipts over the concession come to minus Rs 5,608 crore. Kerala's replies are in the report, and the auditor did not accept them.

The Mundra record concerns environmental compliance. A committee set up by the environment ministry in 2012 reported in April 2013, and in January 2014 the Gujarat High Court ordered the ministry to decide the port's clearance and stopped further work until it did.

3. Coal

The Adani group reaches coal by two routes. It operates mines that state and central utilities own, as mine developer and operator, and since 2020 it has bought mines outright in the Ministry of Coal's commercial auctions.

3.1 Hasdeo Arand

Parsa East and Kanta Basan in Chhattisgarh's Hasdeo Arand forest were allocated to the Rajasthan state generation company in 2007, with an Adani company as mine operator and majority partner in the mining joint venture. On 22 June 2011 the environment ministry's Forest Advisory Committee recommended rejecting the diversion of 1,898 hectares of forest for these blocks. The next day the Minister of State for Environment overruled the committee and granted first-stage clearance. In March 2014 the National Green Tribunal sent the clearance back to the committee and suspended work; that suspension is reported to have been stayed by the Supreme Court. The Supreme Court's 2014 order cancelling coal allocations nationally included these blocks, and the Ministry of Coal's later records treat the same state company as the prior allottee. The second phase received fresh permissions in 2022 and is under challenge in the Supreme Court and the Chhattisgarh High Court.

3.2 Commercial auctions

Adani companies won 8 mines in the Ministry of Coal's commercial auctions between 2020 and 2023. Where the ministry published the number of bidders, most of these auctions drew two. Gondulpara drew 4 qualified bidders and Bijahan six. Gondbahera Ujheni East went to an Adani company on a single bid at the second attempt, with the approval of the Empowered Committee of Secretaries; the ministry records that eleven mines in all were allotted that way across the auctions. In one auction two Adani companies both qualified for the same mine, which another bidder won.

4. The manufacturing-linked solar award

In June 2019 the Solar Energy Corporation of India tendered 8,000 MW of solar power linked to building 2,000 MW of solar manufacturing. The Central Electricity Regulatory Commission's order of 2 April 2022 records that 3. Capacity from the package nobody bid for was moved to the other, and only Adani Green accepted it. The final award was 8000.

No state signed to buy the power until 2021. After the developers cut their tariff to Rs 2.42 a unit, Andhra Pradesh agreed to buy 7,000 MW and its regulator approved the purchase on 11 November 2021. An objector told the Commission that the ceiling tariff had been raised from Rs 2.75 to Rs 2.93 to make room for the manufacturing condition; the Commission declined to examine how the bidding had been structured and adopted the tariffs.

This is the transaction behind the 2024 indictment in New York. What the indictment alleges is set out in Part 3, as allegation. The record above does not depend on it.

5. Godda

Adani Power's 1,600 MW plant at Godda in Jharkhand sells all its output to Bangladesh. The commerce ministry notified a power special economic zone of 222.668 hectares for it in September 2019, which carries the tax treatment of an export unit. In August 2024 the Ministry of Power amended its cross-border electricity guidelines so that a plant built only to export can be connected to India's grid if its foreign buyer stops scheduling power or defaults. Godda is the only such plant the paper found. The earlier steps (the SEZ board's rejection in 2018 and approval in 2019, the Jharkhand agreement) rest on press reports, and are shown as such.

6. Dharavi

The Bombay High Court's judgment of 20 December 2024 sets out how the Dharavi redevelopment was awarded. In the first tender, opened in January 2019, Seclink bid Rs 7,200 crore and Adani Rs 4,529 crore. The state cancelled that tender in 2020, on the ground that railway land had not been part of the bid documents. The fresh tender of 2022 added the railway land and further concessions; 3. Adani was selected at Rs 5,069 crore. The court dismissed Seclink's challenge, and recorded Adani's argument that the two price bids measured different things. A further challenge is reported to be before the Supreme Court.

7. Transmission, where the bidding was close

Not every award was thin. The two transmission auctions examined in full were contested to the last rupee. For the first phase of the Khavda lines, 7, and Adani won at Rs 1,000.33 million a year against Power Grid's Rs 1,002.84 million. At Karur, Adani won at Rs 183.54 million against Sterlite's Rs 184.01 million. Adani's 2018 purchase of Mumbai's electricity business was from Reliance Infrastructure, Anil Ambani's company, and was approved by the Maharashtra regulator.

8. Land at Mundra

A committee constituted by the environment ministry in 2012 reported in April 2013 that 75 hectares of mangrove had been lost at Bocha Island near the Mundra port, and recommended a restoration fund of Rs 200 crore or 1% of project cost. It also recorded that grazing land at Zarpara village had been taken back by the state for the SEZ in 2007. No audit by the Comptroller and Auditor General finding land allotted to Adani at concessional rates was found. One reported audit figure on forest land valuation could not be confirmed from the report itself and is not used.

9. The KG-D6 gas field

Reliance won the KG-D6 block in 2000. Three audits by the Comptroller and Auditor General followed. The 2011 audit found that the whole 7,645 square kilometre block had been treated as a discovery area, so none of it was given back as the contract required; that the development plan's cost estimate rose from US$ 2.4 billion to US$ 5.2 billion plus 3.6 billion; that 10 (8 to Aker Group) high-value contracts were awarded on single financial bids; and that the profit-sharing formula gave the contractor a reason to spend early. The 2014 audit found US$ 10,442 million spent and US$ 9,293 million recovered as cost by March 2013. The 2016 follow-up put excess cost recovery at US$ 1,547.85 million.

The auditor did not find that costs were inflated. It said it could not yet validate them. The separate dispute over gas that migrated from ONGC's blocks went to arbitration, where Reliance won in 2018; the Delhi High Court upheld that award in 2023 and a Division Bench set it aside in February 2025, after which the petroleum ministry demanded US$ 2.81 billion. The appeal is in the Supreme Court.

10. Jio's licence

Jio began as Infotel, an internet licensee that won broadband spectrum in all 22 circles in the 2010 auction for Rs 12,848 crore. In 2013 it moved to a unified licence that allowed voice calls, paying a migration fee of Rs 1,658 crore, the price set in 2001. The Comptroller and Auditor General's Report No. 20 of 2015 found that this gave the company an "undue advantage" of Rs 3,367 crore, because the fee took no account of the time value of money, and rejected the telecom department's reply. TRAI later cut the charge an operator pays another to complete its customers' calls, from 14 paise a minute to 6 in 2017 and to zero from 2021; the lower the charge, the less an operator whose customers make more calls than they receive pays out. The Competition Commission closed a predatory-pricing complaint against Jio in 2017; that order could not be retrieved, and its outcome is shown as reported.

11. Reliance in retail and media

Reliance agreed in 2020 to buy Future Group's retail and logistics businesses for Rs 24,713 crore. Amazon, an investor in a Future company, objected; the Supreme Court held in August 2021 that the emergency arbitrator's order in Amazon's favour was enforceable, and the deal lapsed in April 2022 when Future's secured creditors voted against it. The Disney media merger was approved by the Competition Commission in August 2024 subject to modifications the parties offered.

12. Political finance

The Supreme Court struck down electoral bonds in February 2024 and ordered the State Bank of India to disclose purchases, which the Election Commission published in March 2024. Qwik Supply Chain Private Limited bought bonds worth Rs 410 crore between January 2022 and November 2023. News reports linked the company to Reliance through a director and its registered address; Reliance said it is not a subsidiary of any Reliance company. No official filing establishing or ruling out the link was found, so the paper records the purchases and treats the link as reported. No purchaser in the published list carries the name Adani or Reliance, which does not rule out companies trading under other names.

13. What the record adds up to

Read together, the documents show three patterns. First, several of the Adani group's largest positions came through processes with few bidders or a single one, among them two-bidder coal auctions and a single-bid allotment, a solar tender where one package drew no bids and only Adani took the transferred capacity, and a Dharavi re-tender with two qualified bidders after a higher first bid was cancelled. Second, at decisive points an official body's objection was overruled or not examined, as with the forest committee in 2011, and the bidding structure the regulator declined to review in 2022. Third, where bidding was open and contested, as in the transmission auctions, Adani won on price by fractions of a percent.

For Reliance, the record is older and more about terms than about allocation. It covers the terms of a gas contract the auditor could not validate, and a licence migration the auditor priced at a Rs 3,367 crore advantage.

None of this establishes that any decision was improper. The Bombay High Court upheld the Dharavi award and the electricity regulator adopted the solar tariffs. What the record does establish is that a few public decisions, taken by a small number of officials and committees, did much to shape who holds India's infrastructure, and that the documents behind several of them (the forest committee's reasons, the SEZ board's minutes, the Competition Commission's orders) are not easily available to the public that bears their consequences.
